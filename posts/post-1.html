The 5 Compound Lifts Every Athlete Needs

🏋️

After more than a decade of training and coaching athletes across multiple sports, one truth has held constant: the athletes who build their programs around a handful of fundamental compound movements consistently outperform those who don't. Here are the five lifts I program for almost every athlete I work with.

Why Compound Lifts?

Compound movements recruit multiple muscle groups simultaneously, produce greater hormonal responses, and translate directly to athletic performance far more effectively than isolated exercises. They're also time-efficient – you can achieve more in a 45-minute compound session than in two hours of machine-based isolation work.

1. The Back Squat

The back squat is the king of lower-body development. It builds quad, glute, and hamstring strength simultaneously while demanding core stability and mobility. For athletes, the ability to generate force through a full range of lower-body movement is non-negotiable.

Start with goblet squats to develop the pattern, then progress to barbell back squats. Aim for full depth – hip crease below the knee – before adding serious load.

"The squat doesn't just build strong legs. It builds a strong athlete. Every sport that involves running, jumping, or changing direction benefits from a well-developed squat pattern."

2. The Deadlift

No lift develops posterior chain strength – hamstrings, glutes, spinal erectors, and traps – like the conventional deadlift. It also teaches athletes to produce force from the floor, which directly transfers to sprinting, jumping, and tackling.

3. The Bench Press

Upper-body pressing strength matters in almost every contact sport and many non-contact ones. The bench press builds pectoral, shoulder, and tricep strength while reinforcing scapular stability when performed correctly.

4. The Overhead Press

The overhead press is the most honest test of shoulder strength and stability. It demands full-body tension, trains the rotator cuff under load, and builds the kind of overhead strength that carries over to throwing, swimming, and gymnastics.

5. The Barbell Row

Pulling strength is chronically underdeveloped in most athletes. The barbell row builds upper-back thickness, improves posture, and balances the pressing volume that dominates most programs. Aim for a 1:1 ratio of pressing to pulling in your weekly volume.

Programming Tips

  • Start each session with your primary compound movement when you're freshest.
  • Progress systematically – add weight only when form is solid.
  • Train each pattern at least twice per week for best results.
  • Don't neglect mobility work; it limits how well you can load these patterns.
  • Record your lifts – video feedback catches errors that feel invisible.

Where to Start

If you're new to these movements, hire a qualified coach for even a few sessions to learn the patterns correctly. The time invested in technique early pays dividends for years. A small technical flaw left uncorrected under increasing load is how injuries happen.
