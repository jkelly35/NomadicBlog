The Ultimate Pre-Ski Fitness Assessment

By Joseph Kelly, PT, DPT, CSCS

Ski season is right around the corner, and that means it's time to trade running shoes and bike tires for ski boots and bindings. But before you hit the slopes, it's worth asking one important question: Is your body ready?

Just like tuning your skis before the first big day, assessing your movement, strength, and control can make the difference between peak performance and preventable injury. Below is a simple, evidence-based fitness assessment I use with mountain athletes to gauge readiness for ski and snowboard season.

1. Overhead Squat — Your Ski Movement Screen

If I had to choose one movement to quickly assess ski and snowboard readiness, it would be the Overhead Squat.

The Overhead Squat (OHS) isn’t about strength — it’s about mobility, stability, and control working together. When you raise your arms overhead and squat, your ankles, hips, spine, core, and shoulders must coordinate as one system.

For skiers and snowboarders, that system determines:

  • How well you absorb terrain
  • How stable your knees feel in turns
  • How controlled you are in landings
  • How efficiently you maintain posture at speed

How to Perform It

  • Feet shoulder-width apart
  • Arms overhead (elbows straight, ribs controlled)
  • Descend slowly into a deep squat
  • Perform 3–5 controlled reps
Using a PVC pipe overhead helps standardize the movement. Move slowly — this is about quality, not depth alone.

What We’re Looking For

A strong overhead squat looks balanced and controlled:

  • Heels stay down
  • Knees track over toes
  • Hips reach at least parallel
  • Torso stays upright
  • Arms remain overhead
Common compensations:
  • Heels lifting → limited ankle dorsiflexion
  • Knees collapsing inward → reduced hip control
  • Forward trunk lean → limited thoracic mobility or core stability
  • Arms drifting forward → shoulder restriction
These patterns aren’t diagnoses — they’re signals.

Why This Matters for Skiers & Snowboarders

Research supports the movement relationships we see clinically:

  • Limited ankle dorsiflexion is associated with altered squat mechanics and increased knee valgus (Bell et al., Journal of Athletic Training).
  • Dynamic knee valgus has been linked to elevated lower-extremity injury risk in athletic populations (Hewett et al., American Journal of Sports Medicine).
  • Thoracic mobility influences shoulder and trunk positioning during overhead tasks (Ludewig & Reynolds, JOSPT).
  • Squat movement assessments demonstrate good reliability when standardized (ICC > 0.80 in movement reliability studies).
On snow, this translates to:
  • Reduced ankle mobility → decreased shock absorption in turns
  • Poor hip control → increased knee stress under load
  • Thoracic stiffness → compromised posture and balance
The Overhead Squat doesn’t predict injury on its own. But it highlights movement inefficiencies that often show up before performance drops — or pain begins.

It’s your baseline before you chase vertical, speed, or bigger terrain.

2. Broad Jump — Relative Broad Jump (RBJ)

The broad jump is a classic test of lower-body power, but not all jumps are created equal. To truly compare athletes of different sizes, we use Relative Broad Jump (RBJ)—your jump distance divided by your height. This simple ratio levels the playing field, letting you see how your power stacks up regardless of your build or gender.

How to calculate it: RBJ = Broad Jump (cm) / Height (cm). For example, if you jump 245 cm and are 178 cm tall, your RBJ is 1.38.

What’s a good score? For men, 1.65+ is elite, 1.35–1.64 is athletic, and below 1.20 means there’s room to grow. For women, 1.45+ is elite, 1.15–1.44 is athletic, and below 1.00 is below athletic norms.

Why does this matter? RBJ is closely linked to acceleration, sprinting, and explosive movements in sports like skiing, cycling, and trail running. By normalizing to height, you get a fairer, more actionable measure of your true power.

Research: Broad jump is a reliable, validated field test for horizontal power and sprint performance (Harman et al., J Strength Cond Res; Maulder & Cronin, 2005; Markovic et al., 2004; NSCA Essentials of Strength & Conditioning).

3. Single-Leg Triple Hop — Dynamic Stability Under Load

This is where strength meets control.

The Single-Leg Triple Hop challenges your ability to produce force repeatedly on one leg and stabilize under momentum — just like you do in ski turns and edge transitions.

Hop forward three consecutive times on one leg and “stick” the final landing under control.

Skiing is essentially a series of controlled single-leg load transfers. Every turn requires you to absorb force, redirect it, and stabilize — often at speed and under fatigue. This test shows how well your system handles that demand.

Why It Matters for Skiers

The triple hop measures:

  • Unilateral power
  • Reactive strength
  • Dynamic knee control
  • Landing stability
Research supports its clinical value:
  • Hop tests are widely used in return-to-sport testing after ACL injury due to strong reliability and functional relevance (Noyes et al., Am J Sports Med).
  • Limb symmetry index (LSI) is commonly used to assess readiness and asymmetry risk, with <90% symmetry often considered suboptimal in athletic populations (Logerstedt et al., J Orthop Sports Phys Ther).
  • Single-leg hop performance has demonstrated good test–retest reliability (ICC typically >0.85 in lower extremity functional testing literature).
For skiers, asymmetry matters. Even small deficits can change edge pressure distribution and increase knee loading over time.

How to Interpret It

Measure total distance covered across three hops.
Then compare sides:
Limb Symmetry Index (LSI) = (Weaker Leg / Stronger Leg) × 100

  • ≥ 95% symmetry → Strong bilateral control
  • 90–94% → Mild asymmetry
  • < 90% → Meaningful imbalance to address
A difference greater than 10% between legs may indicate residual strength deficits, reduced neuromuscular control, or elevated reinjury risk — especially in athletes with prior knee history.

The Single-Leg Triple Hop doesn’t just test power.
It tests whether you can control power — which is what skiing actually demands.

4. Lateral Step-Down — Control Under Fatigue

Skiing isn’t just about strength — it’s about maintaining control when your legs are burning.

The 30-Second Lateral Step-Down test measures how well your hips and knees can control repeated load on one leg — similar to what happens during continuous turns, mogul runs, or long descents.

How to Perform It

  • Stand on a 6–8 inch step.
  • Lower the opposite heel toward the floor.
  • Tap lightly and return to full standing.
  • Perform as many controlled repetitions as possible in 30 seconds.
  • Stand fully upright at the top of each rep.
  • The working knee must bend past 90° on every repetition.
  • No partial reps.
  • Test both legs.

What We’re Watching For

This is both a performance and quality test.
Each rep must show:

  • Knee tracking over toes (no excessive valgus)
  • Level pelvis (no hip drop)
  • Controlled descent and return
  • Full knee extension at the top
If form breaks down, reps stop counting.

Why It Matters for Skiers

This test measures:

  • Unilateral quadriceps endurance
  • Gluteal strength endurance
  • Frontal-plane knee control
  • Fatigue-resistant stability
Research supports the relevance of these mechanics:
  • Dynamic knee valgus is associated with increased lower-extremity injury risk (Hewett et al., Am J Sports Med).
  • Hip weakness contributes to increased medial knee loading (Powers, J Orthop Sports Phys Ther).
  • Repetitive single-leg tasks demonstrate good reliability when standardized in lower-extremity functional testing literature (ICC typically >0.80).
In skiing, control under fatigue is critical. As quadriceps fatigue increases, knee joint loading patterns shift — and ACL injury risk rises, particularly late in the day.

If your form collapses at rep 12, it will likely collapse on run 12.

5. Side Plank with Hip Abduction — Lateral Stability Under Fatigue

Skiing is controlled chaos.

Your legs are constantly moving underneath you while your trunk must stay stable. The Side Plank with Hip Abduction tests whether your lateral core and hip stabilizers can maintain alignment under repeated demand.

This isn’t about how long you can hold still.
It’s about how well you can control movement while holding position.

How to Perform It

  • Assume a side plank position (elbow under shoulder, body in a straight line).
  • Lift the top leg 8–10 inches.
  • Lower under control.
  • Perform as many controlled hip abductions as possible in 30 seconds.
  • No sagging hips.
  • No rotating backward.
  • If alignment breaks, reps stop counting.
  • Test both sides.

What This Test Measures

  • Gluteus medius endurance
  • Lateral trunk (oblique) endurance
  • Shoulder stability
  • Frontal-plane pelvic control
These systems are critical for ski edge control and knee protection.

Why It Matters for Skiers

When lateral hip endurance fades:

  • Knees drift inward
  • Pelvis drops
  • Edge control becomes inconsistent
  • Fatigue accelerates
Research supports the importance of these mechanics:
  • Hip abductor weakness is associated with increased dynamic knee valgus (Powers, J Orthop Sports Phys Ther).
  • Frontal-plane trunk control influences lower-extremity loading patterns (Hewett et al., Am J Sports Med).
  • Lateral trunk endurance testing shows good reliability when standardized in clinical settings (McGill core endurance research).
In skiing, lateral endurance isn’t optional. It’s what keeps your knees stacked over your edges when terrain gets unpredictable.

Core endurance keeps your trunk steady during dynamic terrain changes and prevents that late-day fatigue that leads to sloppy turns (and falls).

6. Y-Balance Test — Functional Symmetry & Edge Control

Skiing demands constant micro-adjustments.

Every turn requires you to balance on one leg while the other moves independently. The Y-Balance Test measures how well you can control that single-leg position while reaching outside your base of support.

It’s one of the most researched and clinically respected dynamic balance tests in sports performance.

How to Perform It

  • Stand barefoot on one leg.
  • Reach the opposite foot forward (anterior direction) as far as possible.
  • Lightly tap the ground and return to balance.
  • Maintain control — no falling, no heel lift, no shifting stance foot.
  • Perform three trials and record the best distance.
  • Test both sides.
  • For accuracy, measure reach distance in centimeters and normalize to leg length if possible.

What This Test Measures

  • Ankle stability
  • Hip control
  • Dynamic balance
  • Proprioception
  • Neuromuscular coordination
These qualities are critical for edge control and stability on variable snow.

Why It Matters for Skiers

Research shows the Y-Balance Test is strongly associated with lower-extremity injury risk and movement asymmetry:

  • An anterior reach asymmetry greater than 4 cm has been associated with increased lower-extremity injury risk in athletic populations (Plisky et al., Am J Sports Med).
  • Y-Balance composite scores below 94% of limb length have been linked to higher injury incidence (Butler et al., J Orthop Sports Phys Ther).
  • The test demonstrates good reliability when standardized (ICC often > 0.85 in athletic populations).
For skiers, this translates to:
  • Reduced edge stability
  • Increased knee loading
  • Delayed neuromuscular reactions on uneven terrain
  • Greater fatigue-related breakdown late in the day
If you can’t control a single-leg reach in the gym, controlling a high-speed edge transition on variable snow becomes harder.

How to Interpret It

  • Side-to-side difference ≤ 4 cm → Strong symmetry
  • > 4 cm difference → Meaningful asymmetry
  • Composite score ≥ 94% of limb length → Good dynamic control
  • < 94% → Increased risk profile
The Y-Balance Test doesn’t measure strength alone.
It measures whether your nervous system can control your body when your base of support shifts — exactly what skiing demands.

Interpreting Your Results

If you notice big left-to-right differences, poor control, or limited mobility, don't sweat it — that's exactly why we test. Small corrections now lead to huge gains on the mountain later.

Use these results to tailor your preseason training:

  • Mobility deficits → focus on ankle and hip mobility work
  • Power deficits → integrate plyometrics and strength
  • Stability deficits → prioritize single-leg and core control exercises

Final Thoughts

Skiing is one of the most demanding athletic pursuits out there — it's a blend of power, endurance, balance, and flow. Taking 20–30 minutes for a structured fitness assessment can help you train smarter, perform better, and reduce injury risk once the snow flies.

"The difference between peak performance and preventable injury often comes down to 20 minutes of honest assessment and a willingness to address what you find."
